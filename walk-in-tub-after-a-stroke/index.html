Walk-in tub after a stroke: build it around your stronger side

Door side, controls, water temperature, and when a seated shower fits better.

Quick answer: After a stroke, a walk-in tub only works if it is set up for the side of your body that works best. Order the door and controls for your stronger side, test the water with your unaffected hand, and plan for sitting while the tub drains. Why does a stroke change which tub fits you?

Most strokes affect one side of the body more than the other. That one fact reshapes the whole bath. A leg that drags a little on the affected side has to clear the tub step. A hand that grips weakly has to hold a grab bar or turn a faucet. A patch of skin that no longer feels heat well can sit in water that is too hot without warning you.

Stroke is also common. The CDC counts more than 795,000 strokes in the United States every year, about 610,000 of them first strokes and about 185,000 in people who have had one before. It calls stroke a leading cause of serious long-term disability.

What do stroke bathing guides recommend?

The American Stroke Association's bathing tips and its post-stroke bathing checklist are practical and short. Here is how each recommendation lines up with a walk-in tub, including the one place they pull in different directions.

That sixth row matters. In a regular tub, the water's buoyancy helps you rise. A walk-in tub works differently: the door cannot open until the water is gone, so you sit while it drains and then stand from a chair-height seat. For many people with one weaker leg, standing from a seat at roughly dining-chair height is easier than climbing out of a tub floor even with buoyancy. For others it is not, and only a therapist watching you can say which you are.

Which side should the door and controls be on?

Walk-in tubs are usually offered in a left-hand or a right-hand build. That choice fixes the end the door swings from and the end where the faucet and drain live. After a stroke this is the most important option on the order form, and it is the one people most often leave to chance because the bathroom "only fits one way."

  • Door opening toward your stronger side. Our installers suggest the door open on the stronger side, so you step in leading with the stronger leg and sit with your stronger arm free to hold the bar. Confirm the exact sequence with your therapist, because the right method depends on your balance as well as strength.
  • Controls where your stronger hand reaches from the seat. The American Stroke Association recommends controls you can reach easily; a single lever is easier with one hand than two knobs.
  • A grab bar for the stronger hand at the moment you stand. The bar you use to rise matters more than the one you see in the showroom photo.

If the bathroom layout seems to force the wrong-hand model, ask before you give up. Plumbing can often be moved a short distance, and some models fit either way with a different panel. How do you keep water temperature safe with numb skin?

HealthLinkBC's stroke guidance warns that after a stroke you may not feel temperature, touch or pain on one side, and that if you can't feel heat on the affected side you are more likely to get burned. Its advice is simple: test bath water with your unaffected side and bathe in lukewarm water.

A walk-in tub adds two safeguards worth paying for. An anti-scald (thermostatic or pressure-balance) valve holds the mix steady, and the water heater itself can be turned down. Turning the heater down to 120°F is what the Consumer Product Safety Commission recommends. Its scald chart shows why: water at 140°F can cause a third-degree burn in about 6 seconds, while 120°F takes around five minutes. Our guide to bathing with neuropathy covers temperature checks in more detail, and the same habits apply to one-sided numbness.

When is a seated shower the better choice after a stroke?

The American Stroke Association's checklist says seated showers are safer than baths, and for many survivors we agree. A roll-in or low-threshold shower with a fold-down seat or a shower chair is usually the better fit when:

  • you use a wheelchair, or a walker you need right up to the seat;
  • you cannot yet stand from a chair-height seat without hands-on help;
  • a caregiver helps with every bath and needs room to stand beside you;
  • soaking is not important to you, and getting clean quickly is.

A walk-in tub makes more sense when you can stand from a firm chair with one hand on a bar, you want to soak sore or spastic muscles in warm water, and your therapist is comfortable with a short step. Our walk-in tub fit check weighs these answers for you, and the step-by-step guide to getting in and out shows the seated entry.

What should you ask your therapist before you order?

  • Which leg should lead going in, and which coming out, for a step of about 3 inches?
  • Can I stand from a 17 to 19 inch seat (the ADA reference height for tub seats) with one hand on a bar?
  • Where exactly should each grab bar go for my stronger hand?
  • Is warm soaking helpful or a problem for my muscle tone and blood pressure?
  • Should someone be home for every bath for now, and for how long?

Questions

Frequently asked questions

Is a walk-in tub good for a stroke survivor? ▾

It can be, if you can stand from a chair-height seat with one hand on a grab bar and the door and controls are on your stronger side. If you use a wheelchair or need hands-on help to stand, a seated roll-in shower is usually the better fit.

Which side should a walk-in tub door be on after a stroke? ▾

Most walk-in tubs come in left-hand and right-hand versions. Our installers suggest the door open toward your stronger side, with controls and the main grab bar where your stronger hand reaches from the seat. Confirm the entry sequence with your therapist.

How should I check bath water temperature after a stroke? ▾

Test the water with your unaffected side, since feeling for heat may be reduced on the affected side, and bathe in lukewarm water. A thermostatic anti-scald valve, plus the 120°F heater setting the CPSC recommends, gives you a second layer of protection.

Why can't I leave the water in to help me stand up? ▾

A walk-in tub's door stays sealed until the water drains, so you stand from the seat after the tub empties. That is different from a regular tub, where stroke guides suggest leaving the water in to help lift you. A fast-drain model shortens the wait.

Is a shower safer than a bath after a stroke? ▾

The American Stroke Association's checklist says seated showers are safer than baths. A roll-in shower with a seat is often the better choice for wheelchair or walker users and for anyone who needs help with every bath.
