Walk-In Tub Fit Check: find the tub type that fits your body.

Quick answer: The right walk-in tub depends less on brand than on your body: how high you can step, how easily you stand from a seat, how far your hips and knees bend, and whether you use a walker or wheelchair. Answer nine questions below to see the tub type that fits, or whether a roll-in shower fits better.

Why does body fit matter more than brand?

Most walk-in tub shopping starts with features: jets, heaters, lights, finishes. Those are pleasant, but they are not what decides whether you can bathe on your own next winter. What decides it is a short list of movements: stepping over the threshold, turning, lowering yourself onto the seat, sitting while the water fills and drains, standing back up with wet skin, and stepping out. A tub that makes even one of those moves hard for your body is the wrong tub, however nice it looks.

That is why this page asks about you first. The questions above mirror the checks an occupational therapist or a careful installer makes on a home visit. They are also close to the warning signs in the CDC's Stay Independent fall-risk brochure from its STEADI program, which lists needing to push with your hands to stand up from a chair, and having trouble stepping up onto a curb, as signs of weak leg muscles. Those two movements are exactly what a walk-in tub asks of you every bath.

The bathroom is a reasonable place to be careful. In a CDC analysis of emergency visits for bathroom injuries among people 15 and older, published in MMWR in 2011, 81.1% of the injuries were caused by falls and 68.3% happened in or near the tub or shower, and injury rates rose steeply with age. A tub that fits your body is one practical way to lower that risk. No tub removes it.

How high a step can I manage, and why does it matter?

Every walk-in tub except a transfer model has some step-in height: the lip you lift your foot over when the door is open. It is much lower than the wall of a regular bathtub, but it is not zero, and it differs from model to model. Your step is the first filter, because if you cannot clear it safely you cannot use the tub.

A simple home test is a curb-height step with something solid to hold. If that is comfortable, most seated walk-in tubs will work. If only a low ledge is manageable, ask for the exact step-in height of each model you consider and compare it with a ledge you have practiced on, with someone nearby. If you cannot lift your feet over a ledge at all, skip ordinary walk-in tubs and look at transfer bathtubs or a roll-in shower.

Will I be able to stand up from the seat?

Standing up from a seat with wet skin, after a warm bath, is the moment many people underestimate. Two numbers help. First, measure the chair you stand up from most easily, floor to the top of the seat. Second, find the seat height on each tub's spec sheet. If the tub seat is noticeably lower than your easy chair, you will be pushing harder in a slippery place.

For reference, the 2010 ADA Standards set bathtub seats at 17 to 19 inches above the floor (section 610.2), and the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons suggests a chair 18 to 20 inches high during early recovery from a knee replacement. Private homes do not have to meet ADA rules, but those ranges are a useful yardstick. If you push with your hands to stand, also look at where the grab bar is: you want it where your hand naturally lands as you rise, not behind you.

What if I've had a hip or knee replacement?

Joint replacement changes the answer in two ways. In the first weeks and months, your surgeon's precautions come first: MedlinePlus advises hip patients to keep their hips higher than their knees when sitting and not to sit down in the bottom of a regular bathtub, and many hospital programs use a tub bench or a seated shower until you are cleared. Later, once you are healed, a walk-in tub with the right seat height can be a comfortable long-term choice. Our guide to choosing a walk-in tub after a hip or knee replacement walks through the timeline and the questions to ask your surgeon.

Does a walker or wheelchair change which tub fits?

Yes, mainly through the door. With a walker you need somewhere to park it close to the tub and a clear path to the seat. An outward-swing door usually does that best, because nothing sits inside the doorway, but it needs open floor in front of the tub. If you use a wheelchair and can slide or pivot onto a seat, a transfer tub lets you move across at seat height without stepping at all. The ADA uses a 30 by 48 inch clear floor space as the basic room a wheelchair needs, a handy minimum when you measure. If you need a lift or two people to move, a roll-in shower with a seat is usually the safer and simpler answer.

When is a roll-in shower the better fit?

If you never take baths, if you need a lift or two helpers to transfer, or if you cannot sit still through the time a tub takes to fill and drain, a roll-in or low-threshold shower is often the better buy. Showers have no door to seal and no wait to get out. Aging Safely Baths explains the options in its roll-in shower guide, and you can see complete roll-in shower kits at Showers4Less. We would rather point you to the right shower than sell you the wrong tub.

What should I measure before I call?

  • Your easy-chair height: floor to top of the seat you stand up from best.
  • Your hips, seated: width across your hips while sitting, to compare with seat width.
  • The space: length, width and height of the current tub alcove, and the open floor in front of it.
  • The bathroom door: clear width, since the tub has to come through it.
  • Your step: the highest ledge you can clear comfortably while holding something solid.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

Is this fit check medical advice? ▾

No. It is a planning tool that matches your answers to a tub type. If you have had a joint replacement, a recent fall or a new diagnosis, ask your surgeon, doctor or an occupational therapist to confirm what is safe for you, and follow their instructions over anything here.

What if I cannot lift my feet over any ledge? ▾

Then a standard walk-in tub is usually the wrong fit, because nearly every model has some step-in height. Look at transfer tubs, where an outward door opens down to seat level and you slide across while seated, or at a roll-in shower with a seat.

How high should a walk-in tub seat be for me? ▾

Measure the chair you stand up from most easily, from the floor to the top of the seat, and compare it with the seat height on the spec sheet of each tub you consider. For reference, the ADA standard puts bathtub seats at 17 to 19 inches above the floor.

Is an inward or outward door better for a walker? ▾

Walker users usually do better with an outward-swing door, because it leaves the whole doorway clear and lets the walker stay close. It needs open floor in front of the tub, so measure before you choose. Inward doors suit tight rooms.

Can I use a walk-in tub if I need help standing up? ▾

Often yes, if the seat height suits you and there are grab bars where you push up. Tell us who helps you and where they stand. An outward door and a tub with room beside the seat make assisted bathing easier.
